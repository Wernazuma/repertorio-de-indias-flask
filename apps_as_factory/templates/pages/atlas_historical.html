Historical Methodology

The historical reconstruction navigates the deep disconnect between modern cartography and territorial concepts and the vernacular logics of the 18th century. Rather than imposing an anachronistic “correct” map onto the past or complicating the model in order to represent all conflicting perspectives, HGIS de las Indias aims at offering a sensible version that allows to represent historical data reliably.

The project adopts a “not contradicted” logic. It recognises that colonial space was defined by multiple, often overlapping jurisdictions and inherently metamorphic definitions — “the province ends in the mountains of the infidels” — rather than a single positivistic ground-truth.

This approach is especially necessary given the complications of uti possidetis juris, the principle by which modern nation-states claim sovereignty based on colonial administrative limits as they supposedly stood in 1810. To avoid becoming entangled in these often contradictory national interpretations and their associated “phantom borders”, the project's temporal scope is purposefully capped at 1808. By ending just before the struggles for independence, the infrastructure avoids being pinned to having made a definitive statement on the contested status quo of 1810 — acknowledging that basing rigid modern sovereign territories on colonial territoriality is a cause for perpetuated disputes — regardless of the quality of sources or argument used in the endeavor.

The sections below detail how these fluid historical realities are translated into a structured digital infrastructure.

1. Understanding colonial space

A single entity — “Province X” — could have different spatial footprints depending on whether one discussed civil, military, ecclesiastical, or fiscal matters. To account for these oscillating mental concepts, the project reconstructs space across 13 hierarchical levels (see the technical methodology for how these are modelled).

This lets the system represent the Viceroyalty of Peru in its “broad” and often vague definition — including the autonomous kingdoms of Chile and New Granada or completely unknown spaces east of the Andes — at the highest level, while simultaneously showing its narrower, effective administrative core at the “principal divisions” level. A statistic on the "import of goods into viceroyalty tof Peru" will verly likely not include Chile. Likewise, the Province of Mexico can be shown including or excluding various subordinated autonomous governments and fiefdoms, such as the Marquesado del Valle. By keeping these definitions separate yet related, the HGIS respects the vernacular logic in which boundaries were defined by administrative penetration rather than fixed lines.

2. Territorial reconstruction: from modern municipalities to historical limits

Boundary reconstruction followed a systematic, iterative process that moves from the known modern geography to the interpreted historical one:

  • Initial assignment via modern municipalities. The process begins by identifying the historical towns (pueblos) listed for each jurisdiction in primary sources such as Cosme Bueno or Dorothy Tanck de Estrada. These places are georeferenced and their affiliations compared to the modern municipal territories of nations like Mexico or Peru. Without evidence to the contrary, a modern municipality is assumed to have fallen in a given colonial jurisdiction if its constituent towns shared that affiliation.
  • Resolving split municipalities. Where two settlements within a single modern municipality belonged to different historical jurisdictions, the modern boundary is discarded in favour of the historical evidence.
  • Source-based limit finding. Textual descriptions and cartographic material correct the initial “sum-of-municipalities” logic where mountain ranges, rivers, or other boundary markers are clearly indicated. Even here, administrative reality trumps descriptive definition: if a province ran east until a river, but a town on the east bank was administered by that province, the town is counted as part of it.
  • Heuristic limit finding. We searched textual descriptions and manuscript maps for clues to otherwise-undocumented limits — a mountain ridge, a river, a compass direction. In the Puno region, for instance, the “inner frontier” was reconstructed by drawing a heuristic line where the highlands end and the Amazonian lowlands begin.
  • Comparing place lists and archival material. The methodology relies heavily on comparing place lists across moments in time. By tracking when a settlement appears or disappears from a jurisdiction's census or tax roll, we pinpoint spatial shifts; appointments of officials (nombramientos) fill gaps when jurisdictions were combined or separated.

3. Locating places

Searching for the location/coordinates of the historical places was one of the main tasks in the day-to-day work of the project. To locate places consistently, we had to establish the theoretically “perfect” location for these places and a workable solution for the cases where it could not be established or where we did not invest the time to do so.

  • The exact location should be the (main) colonial church or the plaza de armas of an organized settlement, the seignorial building for haciendas and estancias, any historical nucleus of buildings for less organized settlements.
  • When importing locations from other databases (Tanck de Estrada, Geonames...), use that location unless a special occasion allows/requires checking.
  • Use satellite maps and modern gazetteers as preferred sources, move to locations derived from research texts and maps next, and resort to historical maps/descriptions only where necessary to establish a location.
  • However, if historical documents suggest an error in identity or a relocation of the settlement center, verify and decide accordingly.
  • When a place was located but the identity of a located place is in doubt, flag it. This often happens with minor places that only have a saint name.
  • If a place cannot be located, use dummy coordinates near the closest known place – usually the associated parish or province capital.

4. Resolving contradictions

Sources frequently contradict one another, especially over the assignment of a specific place to a province. The project resolves such contradictions through several strategies:

  • Identifying institutional specificity. Often a place was assigned to a province for one administrative branch only — tribute collection, taxes, or ecclesiastical oversight — while remaining under a different civil jurisdiction. These are not “errors” but reflections of the multi-layered colonial state, which the project captures by assigning the place differently across its 13 levels.
  • Accounting for “distant” sources. Many mistakes were made by contemporaries themselves, particularly in “distant” sources — maps or descriptions produced by someone in a colonial metropolis or in Spain, unfamiliar with daily administration or the geography on the ground. Such a source might place a settlement in the “province of Paraguay” because it lay in the Jesuit province of Paraguay, ignorant that the two do not coincide at all.
  • The “unfalsified interpretation” standard. The database does not claim to be a positivistic ground-truth; it is a not-falsified interpretation. By linking every piece of information to a Source ID, the project stays transparent about provenance, letting peers see which document led to a specific cartographic decision.

5. Logics for resolving ambiguity

To keep the reconstruction consistent and usable, modelling the whole range of ambiguity and uncertainties cannot be the goal of this infrastructure project. It is, however, necessary, that the project follows consistent axioms when certain "arbitrary" decisions must be made, and includes a reduced set of uncertainty markers within the raw tables. Even when these are lost by combining information in the technical workflow, they remain accessible at least in the raw tables. These include:

  • Practice trumps decree. When a territorial change is documented, the date an official actually took office or a reform was implemented is preferred over the date of the legal decree. Many colonial decrees were never enacted, or were delayed for years; many changes were decided in situ and ad hoc, without producing any decree at all. The axiom ensures the map reflects “effective control” rather than “metropolitan dreams”.
  • Continuity and stability. If an information element is identical at two consecutive moments, it is assumed stable in between. The earliest known state is extrapolated back to the start of the project period (1701) and the last known state forward to its end (1808), unless there is clear reason to choose a different date.
